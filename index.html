01 — Origins

What Is Pottery?

Pottery is the art and craft of forming objects from clay and hardening them through fire. It stands among humanity's oldest inventions — a technology so fundamental it shaped how civilizations stored food, carried water, and told their stories.

This transformative craft spread independently across nearly every ancient civilization, from Mesopotamia to East Asia and the Americas — each culture discovering, on its own terms, that raw earth could be made permanent.

clay + heat = ceramic — one of the first human-made materials, and the foundation of ancient civilizations worldwide.

29,000 BC

The earliest known fired ceramic figurines appear in Moravia, in what is now the Czech Republic — clay hardened by flame before it was ever used to hold anything at all.

18,000 BC

The first functional pottery vessels emerge, built for storing food and water — the moment clay becomes infrastructure.

Onward

The craft spreads to every corner of the ancient world, adapted by each culture to its own clay, climate, and ceremony.

02 — Classification

The Three Main Types

Not all pottery is created equal. The three primary categories differ in clay composition, firing temperature, and final character — from rustic earthen vessels to refined, translucent porcelain.

Oldest Type

Earthenware

Porous and warm-toned, fired at the lowest temperatures. The most ancient and accessible of the three, forgiving in the hand and rich in rustic character.

< 1150°C

Porous · low-fire · earthy palette

Everyday Strength

Stoneware

Dense, durable, and non-porous once fired. The workhorse of studio pottery — strong enough for daily use, still warm in tone.

1200–1300°C

Durable · non-porous · mid-to-high fire

Refined Extreme

Porcelain

Translucent, brilliant white, and fired hotter than any other ceramic. Built from the purest clays, it is the most technically demanding to master.

> 1260°C

Translucent · refined · high-fire

03 — Foundations

Raw Materials

Every ceramic begins with the careful selection of raw earth. Primary clays each lend the clay body a distinct property, while temper controls how it shrinks, dries, and survives the fire.

Kaolin

Pure white primary clay — the basis of fine porcelain.

Ball Clay

Adds plasticity and strength to the clay body.

Bentonite

Highly plastic clay, used sparingly for binding.

Temper

Sand or grog — reduces shrinkage and prevents cracking.

04 — Craft

The Process

From raw earth to finished vessel, clay passes through six distinct stages — each one demanding its own patience, temperature, and touch.

Stage 01

Preparation & Wedging

Raw clay is dried, crushed, and re-hydrated to a workable consistency. Wedging kneads out trapped air — preventing cracks and explosive failures later in the kiln — while evening out moisture throughout the body.

01
02

Stage 02

Hand-Building

Pinching presses and rotates a ball of clay into form — the most primitive, meditative method. Coiling stacks rope-like lengths for tall vessels. Slab construction joins flat sheets for angular, architectural pieces.

Stage 03

Wheel Throwing

Centering forces wedged clay into a true, spinning mass. Opening drives the floor and inner walls. Pulling lifts and thins the walls in rhythmic passes. Shaping defines the final silhouette.

03
04

Stage 04

Drying

At leather-hard, clay has lost 15–20% of its moisture — firm enough to trim and join, still flexible enough to shape. At bone-dry, all free moisture is gone and the piece is fragile, ready for its first firing.

Stage 05

Kiln & Firing

Bisque firing at 900–1000°C drives off bonded water and hardens greenware into bisqueware. Electric kilns favor clean oxidation; gas kilns enable reduction atmospheres; wood kilns leave unrepeatable ash and flame marks.

05
06

Stage 06

Firing Temperatures & Vitrification

Heat is tracked with the Orton Cone system. As temperature rises, clay fuses into a dense, glass-like, non-porous state — vitrification, the moment raw earth becomes true ceramic.

05 — Surface

Glaze

Glaze is a thin layer of glass, fused to the ceramic surface by fire. Chemistry and technique together decide whether that surface glows, mattes, pools, or shatters into crystal.

SiO₂

Silica

The glass-former — the core structural ingredient of every glaze.

Flux

Calcium · Potassium · Sodium

Lowers the melting point so silica can actually melt in the kiln.

Al₂O₃

Alumina

Adds stability and viscosity, keeping the glaze from running off the piece.

06 — Heritage

History & Culture

Pottery emerged independently across the globe, each tradition carrying its own technique, symbolism, and sense of the sacred.

Japan · c. 14,000 BC

Jomon Ware

Widely regarded as the world's oldest fired pottery tradition.

Ancient Greece

Athenian Amphorae

Black- and red-figure vessels that doubled as cultural records.

China · Shang & Tang

Celadon Glazes

High-fire stoneware mastery and refined glazing traditions.

American Southwest

Pueblo Coil-and-Scrape

Mineral pigments encoding cosmological meaning in geometric form.

The Spirit of Ceramics

Across every civilization, fired clay transcended utility to become sacred — from Mesoamerican burial urns to ceremonial bowls, pottery carried the weight of belief, memory, and identity.

07 — Expression

Installation works transform gallery spaces, arranging thousands of hand-formed vessels to evoke landscape, loss, or wonder.

08 — Innovation

Modern Technical Mastery

Advanced ceramics have moved far beyond the potter's studio, powering some of the most demanding engineering on — and off — the planet.

3D-Printed Ceramics

Precision fabrication of lattice structures and forms impossible to hand-throw or mold.

Bioceramics

Biocompatible ceramic materials used in surgical and dental implants.

Thermal Barrier Coatings

Aerospace-grade coatings that shield jet engine components from extreme heat.

09 — Mechanism

The Science of Ceramics

Ceramics are more than shaped clay. When fired, clay minerals undergo irreversible change at the molecular level — forming crystalline silicate structures that give ceramics their hardness, heat resistance, and durability.

Molecular Structure

Crystalline silicate bonds formed during firing.

Thermal Properties

Low conductivity, extreme heat resistance.

Material Strength

Hardness, brittleness, compressive resistance.

Vitrification

Glass-phase formation that densifies and seals the body.

10 — Stewardship

Sustainability & Earth

Sustainable ceramics begins with responsible sourcing and a return to older, gentler methods of working with fire and earth.

Local Clay Sourcing

Reduces transportation emissions and preserves regional material traditions.

Natural Ash & Mineral Glazes

Wood ash, iron oxide, and plant matter replace toxic synthetic chemicals.

Reclaimed Clay & Water

Recycling unfired scraps and reusing kiln wash reduces the studio's footprint.
